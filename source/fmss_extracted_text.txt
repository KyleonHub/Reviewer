MIXED SIGNALS AND SENSORS 
What is Amplifier in FMSS? 
 Amplifier is a device or circuit that takes a 
small electrical signal from a sensor or 
mixed-signal source and increases its 
strength (voltage, current, or power) so it can 
be processed, transmitted, or recorded 
effectively. 
IN SENSORS 
 Many sensors (like temperature sensors, 
strain gauges, microphones, photodiodes) 
produce very small signals in the millivolt or 
microvolt range. 
 An amplifier boosts these weak signals to a 
usable level  for analog -to-digital converters 
(ADC) or controllers. 
IN MIXED SIGNALS 
 Mixed signals  involve both analog 
(continuous) and digital signals in the same 
system. 
 Amplifiers are used in the analog front -end to 
prepare sensor signals for digitization or 
further processing. 
TYPES OF AMPLIFIERS 
 Voltage Amplifier – Increases the voltage of a 
weak signal. 
 Current Amplifier  – Increases current for 
driving loads. 
 Operational Amplifier (Op -Amp) – Common 
in signal conditioning for sensors. 
 Instrumentation Amplifier  – Specially 
designed for accurate, low -noise 
amplification of sensor outputs

What is an Operational Amplifier? 
Operational Amplifier 
An operational amplifier, or op -amp, is a very high 
gain differential amplifier with high input impedance 
(typically few megaohms) and low output impedance 
(less than 100 ohms). 
An op -amp contains a number of differential 
amplifier stages to achieve a very high voltage gain.

An OPERATIONAL AMPLIFIER 
(often abbreviated as "op -amp") 
is a high -gain, direct - current 
(DC) electronic amplifier with 
two inputs and a single output. It 
is a fundamental building block 
in analog electronic circuits and 
is widely used in a variety of 
applications due to its versa tility 
and performance characteristics 
 
Of the many linear integrated circuits that are 
available, perhaps the most widely used  (if not 
versatile) is the operational amplifier. It is capable of 
performing a wide variety of linear and non -linear 
amplification and signal processing functions. 
Typical uses of the operational amplifier are to provide 
voltage amplitude changes (amplitude and polarity), 
oscillators, filter circuits, and many types of 
instrumentation circuits.

Ideal Op-Amp Characteristics 
The ideal op -amp would be expected to have the 
following ideal characteristics: 
INFINITE OPEN-LOOP VOLTAGE GAIN 
- This is the voltage gain of the op -amp without 
any external feedback and varies with 
frequency 
INFINITE INPUT IMPEDANCE 
- This is the internal resistance or impedance as 
seen at either the non -inverting input with 
respect to ground while the remaining input is 
grounded. This would show that the op -amp 
ideally does  not require an input current. 
ZERO OUTPUT IMPEDANCE 
- This is the internal output resistance or 
impedance as seen from the output terminal 
with respect to ground. This would show that 
the op -amp ideally could efficiently transfer 
voltage signals from its output. 
The ideal op -amp would be expected to have the 
following ideal characteristics: 
INFINITE BANDWIDTH 
- This is the frequency at which the open -loop 
voltage gain decreases to 1 or 0dB 
- This is sometimes called the unity gain 
frequency/bandwidth. This shows that the op-
amp ideally can amplify any frequency from 
DC to infinity. 
INFINITE COMMON-MODE REJECTION RATIO 
- This is the measure of the ability of the op-amp 
to reject common -mode signals that is 
simultaneously present at both inputs. 
INFINITE SLEW RATE 
- This is the time rate of change of the output 
voltage with the op -amp circuit having a 
closed-loop gain of 1. Ideally, the output 
voltage should exactly follow the input signal 
without distortion. For a rapidly changing input 
signal, such as square wave, t he output 
should also resemble a square wave. 
The ideal op -amp would be expected to have the 
following ideal characteristics: 
ZERO INPUT OFFSET VOLTAGE 
- This is the equivalent DC voltage that must be 
applied to one of the input terminals to 
produce a zero output voltage while the other 
input is grounded. 
ZERO INPUT BIAS CURRENT 
- This is the average of the two DC currents that 
flow into the inverting and non-inverting inputs 
and is the dc current required by the inputs of 
the amplifier to properly operate the first 
stage. 
ZERO INPUT OFFSET CURRENT 
- This is the difference of the two input bias 
currents whose polarity can either be positive 
or negative.

The 741 Op-Amp Maximum Ratings 
These are the maximum values that the op -amp can 
safely handle without the possibility of destruction. 
Under no circumstances should they be equal or 
exceeded. 
SUPPLY VOLTAGE (+VCC, -VEE) 
- This is the maximum positive and negative 
supply voltages that can be supplied to the op-
amp. 
- For the 741 op-amp, the max supply voltage is 
±22 V. 
DIFFERENTIAL INPUT VOLTAGE (VD) 
- This is the maximum voltage that can be 
applied between the two inputs. 
- For the 741 op-amp, this is typically ±30 V. 
COMMON-MODE VOLTAGE / INPUT VOLTAGE (VICM) 
- This is the maximum input voltage that can be 
simultaneously applied between both inputs 
and ground. In general, this is equivalent to the 
positive supply voltage. 
- For the 741 op-amp, this is typically ±17 V. 
These are the maximum values that the op -amp can 
safely handle without the possibility of destruction. 
Under no circumstances should they be equal or 
exceeded.

- This is the maximum power that the op-amp is 
capable of dissipating at a specified ambient 
temperature. For higher temperatures, this 
must be de-rated or reduced accordingly. 
- For the 741 op-amp, this is typically 500 mW.
